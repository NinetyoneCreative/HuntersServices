If you have noticed more spiders around the porch light, in the garage corner, or scooting across the hallway after dark, you are not imagining it. Late August into September is one of the most active stretches of the year for spiders across Northern California, and it happens on a fairly reliable schedule. Here is what is actually going on, and what helps.

It Is Not an Invasion, It Is the Calendar

Most of the spiders you see indoors this time of year are not moving in because your home is dirty or because something has gone wrong. They are simply at the point in their life cycle where they move. Spiders that hatched in spring have spent all summer growing, and by late August the mature ones are on the move looking for mates and for sheltered places to spend the cooler months. Add in NorCal's late-summer pattern of hot dry days and noticeably cooler nights, and the shaded, humid, undisturbed edges of a house start to look pretty appealing.

The other piece is food. Porch lights, landscape lighting and lit windows pull in moths, gnats and other flying insects all evening long. Spiders follow the food. If your porch is the brightest thing on the block after sunset, you are running a nightly buffet, and the webs around the eaves are the natural result.

Where They Are Actually Getting In

Spiders do not chew through anything. They use openings that are already there. In most Oroville, Chico, Paradise and Redding homes we walk, the same handful of spots show up again and again:

  • Gaps under exterior doors where the sweep has worn thin or lifted at one corner
  • Garage doors that no longer seal flat against the slab, especially at the two bottom corners
  • Torn or loose window screens, and screens that sit slightly proud of the frame
  • Openings around dryer vents, hose bibs, cable and utility penetrations
  • Crawlspace and attic vents with damaged or missing screening
  • Firewood, cardboard boxes and stacked storage sitting flush against an exterior wall

What Actually Helps

The good news is that spider pressure responds very well to physical changes around the house. This is the heart of Integrated Pest Management, or IPM, and it is the backbone of our year-round pest control service: change the conditions first, then treat only where treatment is genuinely warranted.

Seal the easy gaps. A new door sweep and fresh weatherstripping on the two or three doors that need it will do more than most people expect. Patch screens while you are at it.

Rethink your outdoor lighting. Switching porch and landscape bulbs to warm amber or yellow tones attracts far fewer flying insects, which quietly reduces the food supply. Moving a light off the wall and onto a pole a few feet away helps too.

Clear the perimeter. Pull firewood, mulch piles, empty pots and stored boxes eighteen inches or more off the foundation. Firewood against the wall is also a common invitation for termite activity, so this one does double duty. Trim shrubs and low branches so they are not touching siding, and keep the trees and shrubs around your home from forming a bridge to the roofline. That strip of clear ground is one of the most effective things you can maintain.

Knock down webs regularly. A long-handled duster or a broom along the eaves, porch corners and window frames every few weeks tells wandering spiders that this is not a quiet place to settle.

Keep the garage tidy. Swapping cardboard for sealed plastic bins removes a favorite harborage and makes the whole space easier to sweep.

When It Makes Sense to Call

Most late-summer spider activity is normal and manageable with the steps above. It is worth having someone take a look when you are seeing spiders indoors consistently rather than occasionally, when webs keep returning within days of being cleared, when you are finding them in bedrooms or children's rooms, or when you are seeing species you would rather not identify on your own. Persistent indoor activity also tends to mean there is another insect population feeding them, and that is usually the more useful thing to find and address.
